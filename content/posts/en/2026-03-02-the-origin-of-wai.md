# Walk this WAI: The Origin

This will be a slightly more didactic and explanatory post, perhaps not as experimental, but that doesn’t mean it won’t be entertaining. My main idea is to share my vision after experimenting for several months with AI, squeezing it to its limit to understand how to get the most out of something we still don’t fully comprehend. And I do this for a fundamental reason: I don’t like programming. I’ve been saying this [publicly for years](https://youtu.be/ioeMeQNEgL8?si=4b66Jm53aEtlQMfr&t=99), yet it remains an essential tool for what I'm truly passionate about: solving problems.

Why don’t I like programming or coding? First, because I’m a slow writer. I’ve improved over time, but my hands are still much slower than the speed of my head. Second, because I have no formal training in systems, which makes everything always seem too complicated. That doesn’t mean I don’t know things: I’ve read dozens of books, watched hundreds of talks, [thousands of articles](https://ak.saxa.xyz/hipervinculos/), courses... But that lack of training turned out to be an advantage, as it allowed me to question every gesture of the process and find ways to ensure things work. Something I’ve learned over time is that the biggest problem with systems isn't the systems themselves, but the people, and it’s always best to try and remove them from the processes[^humanos].

As the cost of generating software collapses, the real challenge is no longer writing the code but **intentionality**. Geoff Huntley explains it very well in his post [The Burrowing](https://ghuntley.com/real/): AI is not just knocking on our door; it’s digging under our foundations. If we don’t mark a clear path of how to work now, we risk leaving an entire generation of less experienced people without a map to navigate this new world where traditional technical skills are being commoditized at an absurd pace. How do we train someone when "cranking out code" is no longer the entry point?

## The Rule of Three and the Path of Least Effort

When I say we need to remove people from the processes, I mean removing the friction generated by our own fallibility. And for that, I ended up defining a rule that helps me prioritize what to do: the Rule of Three. If something happens three times, it’s no longer a statistical anomaly. It’s a sign that the system is screaming for you to automate it so you never have to think about it again.

The clearest example is in those repetitive tasks we do a thousand times a week[^just]. How many times have we made a mistake in a multi-step process just because of a simple memory lapse? Or worse: having an instruction manual that goes out of date in two days? To avoid that, the best practice is for the instruction to be the command itself. If the correct path is the easiest to follow, then it becomes the norm. It’s a way to shield the system against our own limitations and ensure that the order of steps doesn't depend on our memory or our patience that day.

## The Breakthrough: The Open Tap

This same obsession with automating the repetitive led me to my next big personal breakthrough: AI. It arrived like a magical autocomplete that promised a lot, and I had the advantage of experiencing it in a context where we had no limits on what we could consume. I was in a new project that forced me to learn several languages and internal tools at the same time[^tech]. At first, it was overwhelming. As team lead, my task was to see what was possible and where the points were where the tool failed or became dangerous; I felt like someone with a machete clearing a path toward somewhere.

And there was only one way to do it. As Steve Yegge and Gene Kim propose in their book *Vibe Coding*: **Embrace the AI and stop looking at the code.**[^vibecoding]

It was a calculated decision, not a whim. The code, in reality, is not the product itself (we can program in any language); what really matters is that the functionality is correct. The image that came to mind is this: if we want to build high-quality products, whether plastic cups or an F1 car, we need an infrastructure that allows us to do so with the best possible quality. With programming, we can always improve the factory with which we build the product itself, and that is a huge advantage when we know we can do it. As I said before: I don’t like programming, I like creating things and solving problems. And just like the monkeys that use sticks to eat ants[^monos], we can create programs to improve our products.

## The Lab: From Jams to Fabbro

With this idea of the factory in mind, I started experimenting to see how far I could go[^tools]. The browser is the universal interface for almost everything[^talk], so following in the footsteps of Simon Willison, I created small tools that fit on a single page: the **Jams**[^jams]. I started with a URL cleaner and ended up with an application that recorded the cell phone's accelerometer in real time. The premise wasn't to see the quality of what I was doing, but to see how I could create functions and how much time it took me.

But creating functions fast has a cost: review time. The challenge appeared when I tried to accept what the AI proposed without being able to review it easily. When it generated a huge file with a thousand things to look at, where the hell did I put the comments?

That’s how **Fabbro**[^fabbro] was born, a tool that allows me to put notes on the code just like we do in Google Docs. With it, I started testing a way of working guided by specifications[^sdd]: I would define what the program had to do in simple documents and the AI would take care of implementing it. The best part of all was that I could test the same tool while I was building it, defining its own rules. I solved a concrete problem: being able to leave feedback for the machine from anywhere, with surgical precision, without getting lost in the code.

## Nayra and Autonomy

With a review system in place, I started to trust the autonomy of the AI. I no longer just asked for chunks of code; I started organizing tasks for it through tickets[^beads] so it could manage what to do while I only had to supervise. Knowing that with this methodology I could generate complex things in languages I barely knew, I felt emboldened to go further.

Thus was born **Nayra**[^nayra], a visualizer of events on a timeline. I always wanted to contrast events (what musicians existed when Einstein was a kid?), but I never had the urge to sit down and write the necessary JavaScript. This time I only needed to chat quite a bit with the machine to structure the problem and define what functionalities I wanted. The result went beyond my initial idea: from a simple musician comparator, it ended up mapping the times of the universe and a million more details, inspired by a Kurzgesagt video[^kurz] that had fascinated me for years.

## The Birth of WAI

But with each new project, the same tedious task of configuring everything from scratch appeared[^setup]. I had to keep unifying the "skills" of each tool—because each one wants its own configuration files—and the process became repetitive. Besides, I found that by mid-week I was running out of credits everywhere; I had to wait for the next period or keep loading more money... They already had me hooked.

That’s why I created **WAI**[^wai], a tool to make the development process more predictable. The main idea was to standardize all the practices I had learned over the years into something that was "AI-first." It’s not for a human to use directly, but for the AI to use following my rules. I started exploring what I call **AIX** (AI Experience): constantly consulting the AI on what it finds unintuitive or what it needs to work better in other projects.

I even started organizing all the information following a knowledge management method[^para] to see if it was effective for the AI. Over time, I added a subcommand called `way` to verify that the repository had good "internal pressure" practices configured[^pressure], always focusing on creating the necessary infrastructure to generate quality.

## Reflect and Build

One of the commands I use most in WAI is `reflect`[^reflect]. It asks the AI to analyze our previous conversations to detect patterns, vices, or project details that can facilitate future tasks. It’s like having a collective memory that doesn't fail. I’ve never seen a single line of code for WAI; it’s implemented in a language I know nothing about[^rust], but it works and is stable across all platforms.

With all these tools and the experience accumulated, I ventured into a more difficult project: **Fotos**[^fotos]. I wanted an application to take screenshots and annotate them, but with superpowers: text recognition and AI integration for automated annotations. In my operating system, nothing like it existed. In a single afternoon, giving constant feedback to the machine, I solved compilation and packaging problems that would have cost me a lot to solve, and I ended up installing the application on my own computer.

Today, my consumption strategy has stabilized with the Pro plan for 100 USD. It is the most cost-effective in terms of price and quality. I still haven’t reached my weekly budget and I have more and more processes running autonomously. In the end, it’s about raising the level of abstraction: stop worrying about how the code is written and focus on how to structure ideas. It’s what I call **Resonant Coding**.

The code has ceased to be the end and returned to what it always should have been: the means to solve problems. But there is a latent danger, what Steve Yegge calls the *AI Vampire*[^vampire]: the temptation to use that extra productivity to produce ten times more mediocre things and end up exhausted. Now that we can generate functionality in much less time, the true value lies in capturing that time for ourselves. Instead of running faster, we can dedicate ourselves to building higher quality things and, above all, spend more time thinking. Because at the end of the day, AI can execute at top speed, but direction and meaning remain human.

[^humanos]: Removing people from processes isn't about hatred; it's pragmatism. People forget, get tired, make mistakes. Automated code doesn't.

[^just]: Tools like [just](https://just.systems/) allow you to encode these recipes. Some of the best practices I apply include: automated workflows, live environment setup, health checks (doctor), and complex multi-step processes.

[^tech]: At that time, I had to learn Go, TypeScript, BigQuery, and all the company's internal rules at an accelerated pace. AI was the catalyst that made that possible.

[^vibecoding]: *Vibe Coding*, by Steve Yegge and Gene Kim (IT Revolution Press, 2025). The original phrase is "Embrace the AI and stop looking at the code." It is a paradigm shift where the programmer becomes a conductor overseeing intent rather than every line of code.

[^monos]: The analogy of the stick and the ant: we are tool-using animals. AI is simply the longest and most refined stick we've found so far.

[^tools]: At work, we used Cursor but didn't have Claude Code, so I bought the Pro version on my own. I took the opportunity to use Gemini CLI and ampcode (taking advantage of a free credit offer).

[^talk]: I'm referring to *The Birth and Death of JavaScript* by Gary Bernhardt. A gem.

[^jams]: [Jams](https://charly-vibes.github.io/jams/): Single-page web applications inspired by Simon Willison.

[^fabbro]: [Fabbro](https://charly-vibes.github.io/fabbro/): A terminal user interface (TUI) for commenting on code that now also has a [web version](https://charly-vibes.github.io/fabbro-web/).

[^sdd]: Known as *Spec Driven Development* (SDD). In this case, I used files in Gherkin format within a specifications folder so the AI had a clear guide of the expected functionality.

[^beads]: Done through a ticketing system so the AI knows exactly what to work on at all times without losing context.

[^nayra]: [Nayra](https://charly-vibes.github.io/nayra/): A historical event and timeline visualizer that uses OpenSpec.

[^kurz]: [The History of the Entire World](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dGiQaabX3_o), a Kurzgesagt video showing the vastness of time.

[^setup]: I'm talking about configuring tools like beads, openspec, and specific files like AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, or folders like .claude, .amp, and .gemini.

[^wai]: WAI: An agnostic and simple CLI that tries to be the bridge between human and machine.

[^para]: The [PARA Method](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/): Projects, Areas, Resources, and Archives, by Tiago Forte.

[^pressure]: [BackPressure](https://ghuntley.com/pressure/), a concept by Geoff Huntley about infrastructure and software quality.

[^reflect]: A command that encodes years of practice and guides the AI to follow them without me having to remember them all.

[^rust]: WAI is written in Rust, a language known for its performance and safety, but whose syntax I never had the time (or the desire) to learn.

[^fotos]: [Fotos](https://github.com/charly-vibes/fotos): My daily tool for capturing and processing visual information with the help of agents.

[^vampire]: [The AI Vampire](https://steve-yegge.medium.com/the-ai-vampire-eda6e4f07163), by Steve Yegge. A warning on how to use the time saved to think instead of just producing more.
